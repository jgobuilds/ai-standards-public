# Naming playbook

Working reference for Job 5a. Goal: **short, memorable, fun, and ownable.**

The name's job is to be remembered and owned. The tagline explains the product;
the name does not have to. Descriptive accuracy is the most common way people
talk themselves into a forgettable, unprotectable name.

---

## Always present 5–10 options

**Never bring a single recommendation to a naming decision.** Present a slate of
5–10 live candidates, and draw them from **all four wells**:

1. **English words** — including archaic, dialect, and trade vocabulary
2. **Latin** (and Greek) — roots, and words that read as coined
3. **Other languages** — the concept often has a better word somewhere else
4. **Creative combinations** — compounds, portmanteaus, and coinages

A slate drawn from one well is not a slate, it's one idea in eight costumes. If
every candidate is an English noun from the same subject area, go back and
generate again before showing anything.

Show the slate as a table with the source language, the literal meaning, and the
collision and SEO verdict per candidate, so the trade-offs are visible rather
than asserted.

## The bar

Optimize for four things, in tension with each other:

**Recognizability** — it reads as a word, or as something word-shaped, and it
lands on first exposure. Fully abstract coinages start from zero meaning and
need a marketing budget to fill.

**SEO** — the name must be *findable*. Test it as a search query before falling
for it. Three specific killers:
- **Homophones** — a name that sounds like a common word loses the query
- **Existing common nouns** with heavy organic competition (a plant name, a bird,
  a place) — you will never outrank the thing itself
- **Geographic terms**, which drag in unrelated local results

A distinctive coined or compound name is worth real money in search, and this
regularly outweighs a prettier word that is unfindable.

**Readability** — spelled correctly on first hearing, and read correctly on first
sight. Silent letters, ambiguous vowels, and non-obvious stress patterns are
disqualifying however good the meaning is.

**Fun** — has personality. Bland is a failure mode, not a safe choice.

| Test | Target |
|---|---|
| Length | 1–3 syllables. Shorter is almost always better. |
| Radio test | Heard once, spelled correctly without help. |
| Screen test | Seen once, pronounced correctly without help. |
| Search test | Googles cleanly; you could plausibly rank for it. |
| Say-it test | Pleasant and quick to say aloud, repeatedly. |
| Distinctiveness | Doesn't blend into the category's other names. |
| Ownability | Trademarkable; domain and handles obtainable. |
| Scalability | Survives expansion beyond v1's scope. |

**On fun.** A little wit is a genuine competitive advantage because most
competitors won't risk it — categories converge on the same three earnest Latin
roots and all sound alike. Warmth and playfulness are memorable. The limit is
that the joke must not curdle when the product is used seriously, and must not
undercut trust where the product handles money, safety, health, or law.

---

## Generate across strategies

Produce candidates in **every** category below before judging any. Working from
one idea you already like is how shortlists end up with six variations of the
same mediocre name.

**Evocative** — suggests a feeling, benefit, or image without describing the
product. *Nest* (safe, warm home), *Kindle* (igniting knowledge), *Slack*
(informal ease). The sweet spot for most consumer products: memorable, warm,
usually protectable.

**Invented / coined** — entirely made up. *Kodak*, *Xerox*, *Sony*, *Pentium*.
The holy grail of ownability and almost always trademarkable, with the trade-off
that it means nothing until you give it meaning. Strongest when it still sounds
good and hints at something.

**Compound** — two relevant words fused into a new brandable term. *Microsoft*
(microcomputer + software). Clear and ownable when the fusion is clean; clunky
when it's just two nouns jammed together.

**Metaphorical** — borrows a concrete object or role whose properties map onto
the product. Often the best source of oblique names: a tool, an instrument, a
landmark, a role. Concrete beats abstract.

**Oblique** — related sideways rather than directly. Usually the *only* surviving
option in a crowded category, and often the most charming.

**Descriptive** — says what it does. Easy to understand, hardest to protect,
usually already taken, and instantly forgettable. Use only for internal tools or
as the descriptor appended to a real name.

**Other languages, myth, and place** — rich source, but check meaning and
collisions carefully; mythological names in particular are heavily used.

---

## Expect the obvious ones to be gone

**Every name that directly expresses a category's core virtue is already taken**
— usually by firms in that exact category. Words for growth, excellence, the
path, clarity, trust, and insight are exhausted in nearly every professional
market. In one documented naming exercise, six of six candidates in that mold
returned in-category collisions.

Plan for this rather than being disappointed by it. **Names survive by being
oblique or invented.** Budget for vetting a batch of 20–40 candidates across the
strategies above, not for falling in love with three.

---

## Screening

Run in this order, cheapest first — most candidates die early.

**1. Linguistic screen.** Pronunciation and spelling in the primary market;
unintended meanings in other major languages if there's any chance of
international use.

**2. Collision search.** For each finalist:
- Live products in the same category — **disqualifying**, not a hurdle
- The software/tech sector generally, even outside the category
- Trademark registers in the relevant jurisdictions and classes
- Domains and social handles
- **The name plus its descriptor** ("&lt;Name&gt; &lt;Category&gt; System") — a clean
  bare word with a taken descriptor phrase is still a problem

Adjacent-sector collisions are a judgment call. A same-category collision is not.

**3. Audience test.** Recall, preference, and pronunciation with a handful of
real people. Cheap and disproportionately informative.

**4. Preliminary trademark check** before any public artifact exists. Renaming is
a find-and-replace early and a migration later.

---

## Scoring

Score each finalist 1–5 and show the table. Forces the trade-offs into the open
and prevents the loudest advocate from winning by default.

| | Source | Fit | Recognizable | SEO | Readable | Ownable | Fun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Weight **ownable** heavily — an unavailable name scores zero regardless of how
well it reads. Weight **SEO** nearly as heavily: a name you cannot be found
under is a permanent tax on every marketing dollar that follows.

---

## Output

A ranked shortlist with collision status per candidate, the scoring table, a
clear recommendation with reasoning, and the runner-up worth keeping. Record the
check and its date in the dossier's decisions log — including the names rejected
and why, which saves relitigating them later.

If the current working name collides, **say so immediately and plainly**, and
note that renaming is cheapest right now.
